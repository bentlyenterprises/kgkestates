To steward land is to accept that you do not truly own it. You care for it, contribute to it, and try to leave it stronger than you found it. The Glenkindie and Kildrummy Estates are not shaped by a single ambition or aesthetic. Instead, they are guided by a quiet belief: that land thrives when it is respected, not controlled. And that people, in turn, thrive when they’re part of something rooted, alive, and ongoing.

Stewardship here means observing more than managing. It means thinking in decades rather than quarters and asking not only what the land can provide, but what it needs to endure.

The history of this place is layered with centuries of use, from farming to forestry, field sports to feudal ownership. The present is about taking responsibility for that history. Not erasing it, but rebalancing it. Restoring native habitats. Rebuilding degraded systems. And rethinking the patterns of land use that, while once common, are no longer sustainable.

The goal is not to create a wilderness or a showcase. It is to create continuity between land and people, past and future, activity and rest.

Farming

Agriculture has shaped both estates for centuries. Stone walls, grazing fields, and the outlines of old steadings trace a long history of human involvement. That won’t change. Farming remains a key part of the land’s character and use, but how it’s done is changing.

At Kildrummy and Glenkindie, the emphasis is shifting from maximum yield to long-term resilience. This means prioritising soil health, reducing chemical inputs, and restoring field margins and wetlands to support wildlife. Some land remains in productive pasture and hay, while other areas are being lightly rewilded or rotated into conservation grazing.

The goal is not to eliminate farming, but to rebalance it. Grazing livestock still move across the estates, but with greater attention to rotation, pressure, and the effect on surrounding habitats. Native grasses and wildflowers are returning to areas where they’d been crowded out by overuse. In some cases, the best intervention has been to step back, allowing nature to take the lead while monitoring changes with care.

This approach acknowledges that farming is not separate from stewardship – it’s part of it. Well-managed farmland supports biodiversity, stores carbon, and sustains rural livelihoods. The estates are working toward a model that respects all three.

Where possible, local suppliers, graziers, and contractors are employed to keep the benefit of the land’s productivity within the surrounding community. Some cottages are still inhabited by working families who have lived here for generations, and their knowledge continues to inform decisions made today.

Farming is never static. The seasons change, the weather shifts, and the land requires constant attention. But when done in alignment with nature rather than in opposition to it, farming becomes a way of honoring both people and place.

Sustainability

At Kildrummy and Glenkindie, sustainability is not a checklist – it’s a state of mind. Every decision is viewed through the question, What supports the health of this land in the long term?

This means more than protecting views or planting trees. It means rewinding some of the changes imposed in the past, particularly those that prioritised one use at the expense of others. For years, Kildrummy was intensively managed for driven grouse shooting, with predators trapped and habitats tightly controlled. That approach has ended. The balance is beginning to return.

Woodlands once dominated by single-species timber plantations are being carefully thinned and restructured. In many cases, non-native Sitka spruce is being removed to make way for native trees like Scots pine, birch, and rowan – all species that better support biodiversity, hold the soil in place, and restore natural water systems.

Elsewhere on the estate, old drainage systems are being left to slow and settle. Peatlands, long underestimated, are being protected and monitored. Deadwood is left in place to shelter insects. Edges are allowed to fray. This kind of sustainability doesn’t always look tidy. But it works.

Clean energy is also part of the picture. Kildrummy’s wind farm continues to operate, feeding renewable power into the grid and helping Scotland meet its climate targets. The estate’s vehicle fleet now includes electric options, and infrastructure is evolving to support further transition.

Perhaps more than any single initiative, sustainability here is about perspective. It’s about recognising that land is dynamic and that every action creates ripples. The estates strive to move slowly, transparently, and with humility, knowing that restoration isn’t a straight path, and that care for the land includes care for those who live nearby.

That’s why community benefit remains a cornerstone of the estates’ sustainability work. Local employment has increased since the estates changed hands. Contracts are awarded locally when possible. Heritage buildings are being restored not just for aesthetics, but to remain usable for generations. Garden tours and public events open the gates – not just to guests, but to neighbours.

In a broader context, this is part of a national conversation about land ownership, access, and responsibility. While questions around who owns the land are complex and important, what matters most is how the land is used, and who benefits from that use. The estates are committed to answering that question with actions, not slogans.

The Path Forward

Stewardship doesn’t begin or end with a single plan. It’s a relationship between people, place, and time. The work at Kildrummy and Glenkindie is ongoing, sometimes quiet, often unseen. It unfolds in daily tasks like monitoring saplings, mending fences, clearing ditches, and recording wildlife sightings.

There are no illusions here about perfection. Mistakes will be made. Weather will intervene. The land will surprise. The commitment is clear: to listen closely, act thoughtfully, and pass on a landscape that’s more alive than it was found.

That, at its heart, is what stewardship means. Not dominion. Not display. Just the steady work of tending to something that matters, because it always has, and always will.
