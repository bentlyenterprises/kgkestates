Set just miles apart in the upper reaches of Aberdeenshire, Glenkindie and Kildrummy are two estates bound by shared values: a respect for heritage, a commitment to restoration, and a belief that the land holds stories still unfolding. Though shaped by centuries of history – from medieval strongholds to Edwardian gardens – their current chapter is one of care, renewal, and quiet stewardship.

Each estate carries its own character. Kildrummy feels expansive, layered with dramatic history and known for the enduring presence of its ruined castle. Glenkindie, meanwhile, offers a softer rhythm: pastoral and wooded, with stone cottages and timeworn paths woven through its fields. Together, they reflect the diversity of the landscape and the evolving relationship between people and place.

Deep Roots

The past is never far beneath your feet here. Kildrummy Castle, built around 1245, once guarded the route north through the Cairngorms and served as the seat of the Earls of Mar. It witnessed sieges, political upheaval, and shifting fortunes over centuries. Though now in ruin, its remains still command the landscape, watched over by Historic Environment Scotland and open for visitors to wander.

Nearby the early 20th-century Kildrummy Castle Hotel, designed by Alexander Marshall Mackenzie, introduced a new era of Edwardian elegance. It echoed the grandeur of the past while embracing a modern sensibility. That same balance continues to shape the estate’s approach today.

Glenkindie Castle, while less widely known, is no less storied. Its current structure reflects centuries of evolution, with foundations that stretch back to the medieval period. Nestled in rolling fields and mature woodlands, it remains a centrepiece of the estate: quiet, dignified, and full of history. The surrounding land, once cultivated and grazed, has supported generations of working families, and many of its original buildings still stand today, now carefully restored and in active use.

A Working Landscape

The Glenkindie and Kildrummy Estates are not museum pieces, but living places. Fields are still ploughed. Trees are still harvested. People still work the land. At Kildrummy, over 5,600 acres stretch across farmland, forestry, rivers, and moor. Nearly 2,100 acres are dedicated to agriculture, with another 1,300 in managed woodlands. The land supports a diverse range of uses, from hay and pasture to commercial timber and amenity plantings.

For decades, the estates also operated as traditional sporting grounds, with grouse shooting, pheasant drives, and deer stalking long embedded in the calendar. While these activities shaped much of the land management over the past century, the present approach has shifted. Sport shooting has ended at Kildrummy. Instead, attention has turned to biodiversity, natural regeneration, and rebalancing ecosystems that were once tightly controlled to favour a single species.

Salmon and trout fishing continue along a four-mile stretch of the River Don, where efforts are underway to maintain water quality and improve spawning grounds. In the uplands, stands of non-native Sitka spruce are being carefully removed to make room for native woodland – slower growing, more diverse, and far better suited to the wildlife that once thrived here.

At Glenkindie, the land use has long been more domesticated: a mix of residential dwellings, grazing fields, and modest woodland. Yet even here, change is visible. Sustainability-focused renovations and adaptive reuse of existing buildings reflect a growing commitment to efficiency, character, and long-term resilience. Farming continues, but with an eye toward regenerative practices and community involvement.

The estates also embrace a 21st-century dimension: clean energy. At Kildrummy, a cluster of eight wind turbines generates 18.4 MW of electricity, feeding renewable power back into the grid. It’s one more way the landscape continues to serve, not just as heritage or habitat, but as a working contributor to Scotland’s future..

Stewardship and Renewal

What defines the estates today is not ownership, but approach. Since acquiring Glenkindie and Kildrummy, Christopher and Camille Bently have set a course grounded in restoration, not reinvention. The work here is quiet, deliberate, and long-viewed. Rather than reshaping the land to suit a vision, the goal has been to let the land reshape itself through time, care, and a reduction in interference.

At Kildrummy, that has meant banning the trapping, snaring, and predator control once used to maintain conditions for driven grouse shooting. It has meant keeping careful records – not to celebrate the past, but to reckon with it. Decades of "vermin" logs, listing everything from foxes to birds of prey, now serve as a reminder of what’s been lost and what could yet return.

Efforts are underway to restore native woodlands, which support a far greater range of biodiversity than the monoculture timber plantations that once dominated the landscape. Where possible, regeneration is allowed to occur naturally. Elsewhere, strategic planting supports key habitats and encourages the return of species long absent from these hills.

At both estates, historic buildings have been repaired with restraint, preserving what’s useful, discarding what’s not, and ensuring that the original character remains intact. These aren’t showpieces. They’re part of a working whole.

What’s equally important is what remains visible to the local community. The estates are not managed behind closed gates. Public access is encouraged where appropriate. Gardens are opened for visiting. Cottage rentals offer opportunities for deeper engagement. And the work of rewilding, renovating, and renewing is shared not as marketing, but as conversation – open, evolving, and rooted in place.

The Estates Today

Walk the grounds of either estate today and you’ll find a mixture of the enduring and the new. Workshops, outbuildings, and modern utility spaces support the everyday rhythms of the estate: land care, repairs, habitat management, and seasonal work. There’s also a deer larder, a gym, garages, and staff infrastructure. Unromantic, perhaps, but essential for a place that still functions day to day.

At Glenkindie, the cottages are fewer, the mood quieter. Residential and agricultural buildings dot the estate, many dating back decades, even centuries. The surrounding gardens are open for public visiting on certain dates, offering a chance to explore topiary, heritage plantings, and a well-loved pond watched over by a 17th-century dovecot.

Both estates sit close to rivers, roads, and hills, but feel far from anything hurried. The land supports work, yes – but also restoration, retreat, and reflection. These are not estates frozen in time. They are being lived in, nurtured, and shaped with care.

In the Long Term

There is no master plan carved in stone for what comes next. And that’s the point. The work here is seasonal, responsive, and rooted in listening to the land, the wildlife, and the people who live nearby.

In the years ahead, the estates will continue to focus on long-term ecological health. More native woodlands. More resilient water systems. Fewer fences. Greater access. The goal isn’t to create a spectacle, it’s to create continuity. For species. For communities. For the land itself.

Whether you're here as a visitor, a neighbour, or a curious observer, the door is open. This is a place with deep roots and living edges. A place with history behind it, and space still to grow.
