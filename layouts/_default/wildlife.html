To walk the land at GlenKindie or Kildrummy is to be reminded of what’s still wild in the world. Among the hedgerows, high grasses, and river bends, there’s movement. Birds call overhead. Hares dart from cover. Sometimes, if you’re lucky, the hills might be spotted with deer. The estates are not a sanctuary or a safari, but rather a place where wildlife is given the time and space it needs to thrive.

Caring for that wildlife means working with the land, not against it. Accepting its complexity might even mean stepping back at times. Above all, the approach here is about noticing what’s present, what’s missing, and what’s changing slowly over time.

Deer and Balance

Deer have always been part of this landscape. Red deer, in particular, are a keystone species on the estates. Their grazing shapes the structure of plant communities and their movements influence bird nesting, forest regeneration, and even water flow. However, if left entirely unchecked, their numbers can grow beyond what the land can support.

That’s why careful deer management is not just accepted here – it’s essential. At KGK Estates, culling is approached with clarity and care. It is never indiscriminate, and it is never about trophy or tradition. It is about allowing natural systems to recover from decades of imbalance. Grazing pressure is monitored annually by staff and external ecologists. When signs of overbrowsing appear – suppressed saplings, thinned heather, trampling of sensitive ground – the cull is adjusted accordingly. At the same time, when regeneration is robust and habitats begin to balance, that too is reflected in management decisions. The goal is not fewer deer for the sake of it. It’s the right number of deer for the place, at that time.

Stalkers follow best practice guidance in animal welfare, public safety, and hygiene. Non-lead ammunition is used to prevent contamination of venison and minimize harm to scavenging wildlife. In some cases, carcasses are left on the hill. This is both to avoid the damage that extraction vehicles can cause, and to support other species, including raptors and invertebrates.

Wherever possible, this work is coordinated with neighbouring estates. Wildlife does not respect boundaries, and neither should good stewardship. Collaborative management is part of the long-term plan.

What will never change is public access. Visitors are always welcome. Deer management is planned carefully around walking routes and viewing areas, with safety protocols in place to ensure that guests and wildlife alike are respected.

Wildlife in the Margins and Midst

Deer may shape the land, but they’re not its only residents. On any given day, the estates are home to a wide array of species, some common, some rare, and many only visible to those who move slowly and quietly.

Black and red grouse gather in heather and rough pasture. Goshawks and merlins hunt along forest edges. Golden eagles and buzzards wheel overhead, while peregrines carve through the sky in sudden, silent arcs. Pine martens move through woodland fringes. Mountain hares, their coats shifting with the seasons, leave prints across snow-covered ground. Brown hares graze at dusk near the cottages.

Red and roe deer are most often seen at dawn and dusk, especially near woodland edges and open glades. Their presence is both ordinary and quietly moving, a reminder of the wildness that still breathes here.

Across the estates, habitats are being restored with these species in mind. Native trees are replanted and margins are left untidy. Ditches and drains, once cleared for grazing, are being re-wetted to support amphibians and aquatic insects. Predator control has ceased, and old records of "vermin" – once including everything from foxes to wildcats – are now used not as justification, but as a measure of what was lost.

Wildlife monitoring is carried out annually in order to paint a picture of what’s returning, what needs support, and what role the land itself is playing in regional biodiversity year after year. While the team includes staff ecologists and external specialists, many of the most important observations come from everyday encounters: a nesting curlew, a fox track, a sudden burst of lapwing song across an open field.

River Life

At the southern edge of the estates, the River Don moves steadily eastward. In some stretches it is fast and bright, in others slow and reflective, edged with alders and whispering reeds. Across 7.5 miles of double-bank and nearly 5 miles of single-bank access, the river supports a remarkable range of life, both seen and unseen.

Atlantic salmon still migrate through these waters, joined by brown trout, dippers, ospreys, goosanders, and the occasional otter. Beneath the surface, a whole world of invertebrates plays out, from stoneflies to caddis larvae, all essential to the wider food web and indicators of clean, well-oxygenated water.

To help that system flourish, farming has been gradually moved back from the river’s edge. New buffer zones allow native vegetation to establish, stabilizing the banks and filtering runoff. Tree plantings and naturally fallen wood are used to slow the flow, creating microhabitats and promoting natural meandering. On smaller tributaries, similar improvements are underway, restoring complexity where once there was only straight line and speed.

Fishing is still permitted and remains a cherished activity for many guests and locals. But it is now managed not for volume, but for the health of the fish, the river, and the people who spend time there. Seasonal tickets are available, with catch limits and guidelines designed to minimize stress on wildlife and protect spawning areas.

The river, like the land around it, is not static. Its banks shift and pools deepen. It floods, it recedes, and it changes. But with care and attention, it can remain resilient and alive to the generations that will follow.

A Place for Nature, Not a Park

This isn’t a nature reserve. It isn’t a rewilding project with a corporate plan or a final vision board. It’s a working landscape in recovery. Some areas will always be wilder than others. Some will need years of quiet care to return to balance, but all of it is treated with attention and respect.

Visitors are encouraged not to rush. The best encounters – like the best restoration – don’t happen on a schedule. Bring binoculars, walk at different times of day, and listen more than you look.

There are no signs marking every bird species, no feeding stations or guided hides. Just the chance to share the space with something other than yourself.

Looking to the Future

The work of caring for nature doesn’t end with a checklist or a planting map. It’s in the conversations, the tracking surveys, the pauses taken to watch a flock land or a dart between stones.

At KGK Estates, the aim is to create the conditions where wildlife can return, not by force, but by invitation. That takes time, humility, and the willingness to value what's difficult to measure. The land here is not perfect, but it is alive. And that, more than anything, is what we’re working to protect.
